The Coaching Report Card

Michael's question: "Coaching is a tough one like how do teams grade that?" Here is what the data can and cannot grade so far.

Nick Gurol & Michael MacKelvie · August 2026

You asked how anyone grades coaching. It turns out I had been quietly building the answer since July, and none of it has made it into a text until now. The TLDR:

  • The best play-callers are more predictable, not less. Control for situation and the correlation flips sign. Andy Reid gets guessed three extra times per hundred plays and runs the best offense in football. Ben Johnson is the one real exception.
  • Your Seahawks hunch holds up three different ways. Vegas priced them at 10.8 wins, they won 14, third biggest beat in football. Madden rated the roster 23rd of 32 and priced 7.5 wins. Draft pedigree had them 19th, with the league's biggest gap between talent and results.
  • The warning label: beating the spread does not repeat (r = -0.03 across 654 coach-seasons). The market already prices the coach, so the roster models below are the better long-term measure.
  • The career leaderboard passes the smell test. Wins above what the roster predicts: Belichick +4.2 per season, then Dungy, Harbaugh, Payton. Macdonald sits at +1.5 on two seasons, real but preliminary.
  • Andy Reid is not a clock-management disaster. He has the best large-sample record at not dying with timeouts: 4 of 61 close losses vs a 15 percent league rate. The real sinners are Norv Turner (11 of 31) and Mike Martz (double the league burn rate, every season).
  • Dead hypotheses stay on the board. Icing the kicker: 26 seasons cannot prove it works, and Pete Carroll's apparent edge is the fluke screening 21 coaches predicts. Establish the run: zero in every metric, including its three best counterarguments. Halftime adjusters: the skill repeats on paper but correlates 0.81 with simply having the better team.
  • The strangest one: a roster's draft pedigree explains essentially nothing about how the team performs (r = 0.00 across 736 team-seasons). Payroll does better (r = 0.30), and Seattle beat that meter harder than anyone too, ranked 30th of 32 by paid talent.

Everything below is one board. Each card is a line from our texts, then what the data says, then the chart that answers that exact question sitting under it. Open questions say so and name the next move.

Every question, and what answers it

The sequencing file, 18 August

Nick's theory: "we have a theory that ben johsnons play sequencing is unique." Plus Michael's pre-snap question, his U-shape theory, his post-snap rotation questions, and the defensive side. What survived: the chain finding, the play-action volume, a McVay-only motion effect, a rising-rotation trend, and two leaderboards. The dead tests stay on the board with the evidence.

His U-shape theory, testedNo U. Something better underneath.

Michael: "Predictability is U-shaped. You don't want to be at the extremes…we are narrative pattern seeking creatures - if you are too unpredictable, then the narrative does not make sense, it's nonsensical. Your team needs a narrative and structure, but if you're too predictable, the other team will gain an edge on you." And: "So we need ways that McVay, Schnahan, McDonald, and Johnson are like this"

The U is not there, on any axis. Situational guessability, sequence guessability, defensive blitz guessability: three tests, and in each one the quadratic term fails, the information criterion prefers the straight line, and the flexible fit collapses onto it. Offense is monotonic (more guessable tracks better offense the whole way); defense is flat. But the second half of his sentence survives on its own: structure. Every elite name has a strong situational identity. The difference is what the opponent can do with it, and there are two roads: McVay and Shanahan run strong identities that are also guessable, and win anyway. Johnson runs the strongest identity in the sample with the least exploitability in the league, on both guessability axes. His ideal quadrant exists; it just has one man in it.

The catch: the structure measure's raw range is narrow (percentiles spread it out, the underlying differences are small), and Macdonald is placed with defensive analogues rather than the same measures, illustrative rather than literal.

Post-snap movement: right on the trend, empty on the payoffHis guess, checked both ways

Michael: "I wonder if post snap movement out of shell has increased over time…my guess is yes" ... "Also curious if EPA is down on plays where there is a rotation post-snap. That'd tell us if it's worth it."

Right on the first, and honestly empty on the second. Shell rotation rose from 29.1% of dropbacks in 2022 to 32.3% in 2025, climbing about a point a season with the interval clear of zero, and the charting's missingness is under 1.1% and stable, so the trend is real. The worth-it question comes back a genuine null: after situation controls, rotated snaps cost the offense one hundredth of a point of EPA with an interval spanning zero, in every split. Rotating is free, not proven profitable. Macdonald rotates 6 points more than his situations call for, 9th of 41 coordinators, and the trait persists at r = 0.91, the strongest repeatability number on this board.

The film reel: his ten worst-for-the-offense rotated snaps are tabled with date, quarter and clock in data/derived/macdonald_reel.csv. The top three: a Week 10 2025 sack-fumble touchdown against Arizona, the Week 10 2023 pick-six against Cleveland, a second Arizona sack-fumble the same game. Fifth on the list, on merit: the Super Bowl interception of Drake Maye, fourth quarter, 8:49, open shell shown, closed coverage played. Checked against nflverse: Seattle 29, New England 13.

The catch: the reel ranks by outcome, and outcomes on single plays are mostly the offense's mistake plus luck; the rotation set the trap on these plays, but no single play proves the trap did it.

Johnson's chain has no linksThe theory survives, in one specific sense

The theory: "ben johsnons play sequencing is unique"

It is, in what it withholds. Measure how much a defense learns about the next call from the previous one, beyond the situation, and Ben Johnson gives away the least of any of 73 play-caller careers since 2015. Dead last, 2.3 standard deviations below the league mean, and it holds in every one of his four seasons, in Detroit and in Chicago. The average caller hands a defense about 1.2 extra correct guesses per hundred plays through his sequences; Johnson hands over 0.7. His calls are close to memoryless: knowing what he just did tells you almost nothing about what he does next.

The catch: rank 1 of 73 is real and repeated, but he does not clear a formal outlier test; Chip Kelly sits close behind. "Most extreme in the league" is the defensible phrasing, "in a class of his own" is not. Four seasons is still a short career.

Pairs with the predictability cards below: situationally he is roughly league-normal to slightly hard to read. The sequencing is where he is genuinely extreme.

Nobody cashes the setup, him includedNull, for everyone

The romantic version of the theory: he runs early to set up the kill shot later.

Tested per caller, it exists for no one. A pass after a run in the same drive, at fixed situation, gains nothing across 95 caller careers. Eight callers clear their own noise band where screening 95 people hands about five to luck, and Johnson is not one of them; his setup effect is a rounding error with a wide interval, mid-pack in both directions. The league finding on the establish-the-run cards holds at the individual level too: the setup play is a story coaches tell, not a thing the data can find anyone doing.

The opening script is mostly a mythWeak trait, and he does not have it

The other romantic version: the scripted first 15 plays are where the sequencer shows his hand.

Two results, both deflating. First, script skill barely exists as a trait: a caller's script edge this season predicts next season at r = 0.15, with an interval that nearly touches zero. Second, Johnson's own script edge is ordinary, 19th of 64, and his one big script year was his rookie season calling Detroit; it has been slightly negative every season since, including Chicago. The genuinely interesting pattern runs the other way: the famous scripters, Reid, Payton, McVay, Shanahan, LaFleur, all get harder to guess during their scripts. Johnson does not, because he has nothing to switch on. He is one of the hardest offenses to read in the league in the script, after the script, all game.

The catch: script versus rest is not a randomized comparison; script plays live earlier in closer games by construction. And his own confidence intervals are the widest of the marquee names, on 74 games.

His one true outlier ingredient: play action, constantlyNarrows the theory usefully

The question: if his sequencing is unique, it should show up in what he calls.

Across six charted ingredients, exactly one is extreme. Early-down play action: 39.5% of his early downs, second of 35 callers in the charting era, against a league median of 31%. Everything else is league-normal or below; he uses less RPO and less no-huddle than the median caller. Two footnotes worth a beat in the video. His trick plays are only fifth by rate but carry the best payoff in the sample, over a full point of EPA each. And league-wide, play action works slightly better cold than after a successful run, one more nail from the establish-the-run coffin arriving by a new road. His own cold-versus-warm split is indistinguishable from zero.

The catch: the same-look, different-play version of the theory tested null. His run/pass/play-action mix out of a given pre-snap look ranks ninth of 35 in diversity, and the interval overlaps the league median; the only pockets where he is clearly more diverse are second-and-short and third-and-medium.

Does motion blind the model? For McVay, yes. For Shanahan, no.Your question, answered with a model

Michael: "What ways do you think we can quantify the McVay pre-snap movement and Schnahan?"

Nick, in the thread: "Does the model struggle to predict when those two have different motion types pre snap. Struggle to predict run v pass, play action etc."

Built exactly that, and it splits the two names. A pre-snap model reads run versus pass at AUC 0.84 across 139,311 plays. On McVay's motion plays it genuinely struggles: his motion-versus-still gap is sixth largest of 37 callers, and his interval clears zero comfortably. Shanahan, third-heaviest motion user in football, shows no such effect: below the league median, and his motion plays actually produce slightly worse EPA than his still ones. McVay's motion is camouflage; Shanahan's is choreography, moving blockers to where the play is going rather than hiding what it is. Johnson is unremarkable on this test too, which fits everything above: his encryption is in the sequence, not the pre-snap theater.

The catch: FTN's motion flag is presence-only. Jet, orbit and a simple shift all code identically, so this tests whether a caller motions, not which kind, and Michael's "different motion types" question stays open until a motion-type source exists. Sumer's play-level data has no motion type either; checked.

The looks, and the movement inside themMcVay quantified, honestly

Michael: "Ya the McVay motion and movement stuff is like the next part of my writing so let me know when you have something here thanks"

Built from Sumer's per-player data, 4.8 million rows, first analysis on it. Nobody's data counts motions, so this measures the closest honest thing: how many different pre-snap looks a caller shows (every skill player's alignment, per play) and how much the look tips the coming play. Plain words on purpose; these are our measures built on Sumer's alignment charting, not Sumer stats. McVay's result is the surprise that makes his whole reputation make sense. He shows the 32nd-most-varied look of 36 callers, lives in bunched sets (55% of snaps against a league at 39%), rotates personnel the least of the three names, and his looks tip the play more than anyone's, first of 36 at revealing run versus pass beyond the situation. Put that next to the motion card above, where his motion is the one in the league that genuinely scrambles the pre-snap model, and the McVay formula is suddenly coherent: a small set of recurring, individually leaky shapes, with players moving inside them until the leak is bought back. The movement is not many looks. It is life inside a few.

The other two: Shanahan is the mirror: third-most-varied look in the league, middle of the pack at tipping the play, deception by menu rather than by motion, which matches his below-median motion effect above. And an honest correction on Johnson: from the coarse look (backfield count, QB spot, motion flag), R/14 found he hides his mix well; on this richer per-player look he is above median on variety and above median at tipping the play. Different look, different answer, both on the board.

The catch: leak effects are real but small in absolute bits, in line with every tell measure on this board, and the no-motion-data disclosure applies throughout: this quantifies the looks, not the motion itself.

Defensive sequencing: everyone runs a little hotThe other side of the ball

Nick: "im also curious about defensive play call sequencing too" ... "in different game situations etc."

Defensive callers have the opposite signature from Johnson. Blitzing the last snap makes blitzing this snap more likely for nearly every coordinator, beyond what the situation explains, by three to four points league-wide. Nobody meaningfully mixes against his own tendency. The surprise at the top: Vic Fangio, the league's disguise reputation, is among the heaviest chainers alongside Josh Boyer and Don Martindale, with Flores close behind; Macdonald sits toward the mixing end, 35th of 44. And this is a trait of the man, not the roster: a coordinator's situation-adjusted blitz identity repeats between his own odd and even seasons at r = 0.78. The payoff question still comes back empty, though: how guessable the blitz call is has no detectable relationship with the EPA a defense allows (r = 0.08, interval well across zero), unlike the offensive side where predictable calling tracks better offense.

The aside worth a beat: Fangio and Macdonald are both more guessable than the median on the bare blitz-or-not decision, and Fangio chains his calls on top of it. The disguise reputation lives in the coverage shell after the snap, which the disguise cards below measure, not in whether the rush is coming.

The catch: the Sumer charting window is 2022 to 2025, so this is 44 coordinators with 700+ charted dropbacks and short tenures excluded, and the payoff correlation is two noisy per-coach estimates with a wide interval; read the null as "no signal found," not "proven zero."

Macdonald calls one game, everywhereA signature, not a flaw

The situational question: which coordinators call a visibly different game by spot, and which call the same one everywhere?

Macdonald is at the flat extreme, twice over. Across six situations (early down, late down, red zone, two-minute, trailing, leading) his blitz rate barely moves: 33rd of 42 coordinators in situational range, and below the league's rate in all six cuts. And by personnel, his 2023 Baltimore defense reshuffled between early and late downs less than all but one of 121 coordinator-seasons in the charting window, with 2022 also on the static side; his Seattle defenses sit at the league median both years. The flat profile is not a weakness on the evidence: those Baltimore and Seattle defenses finished second in the league in year two, per the card below from his outline. He does not morph by situation; he runs one scheme and runs it well.

The catch: the personnel index is descriptive, with no interval attached, and it cannot separate a coordinator's philosophy from the roster he inherited; Seattle's move to median under Macdonald may be the players, not the plan. Worth knowing for trust: this measure was built twice, once from team-level tendency tables and once from the coordinator's own play-level snaps, and the Baltimore-static, Seattle-median story held both times, though which Baltimore year is the extreme moved (the play-level version, shown here, is the one to trust).

The Shanahan dossierBoth his claims verified

Michael: "What is our story on Schnahan? What makes him special? Do we have anything data wise on him? I have the obvious 'their WR1 was a fuckin runninback' and 'Mac Jones was just as good as Purdy' etc etc"

Both of his examples check out, and the second is the story. McCaffrey out-targeted the actual WR1 by 45 targets, up 46 percent, with 1,029 receiving yards; not close. And Mac Jones against Purdy in 2025 is statistically indistinguishable on 334 versus 363 dropbacks, with Jones's point estimates a hair higher on completion rate and EPA per dropback. One season of a backup's snaps, so suggestive rather than proof, but there is no gap to explain away.

What the board says makes him special, assembled: the system, not the adjustment. His players grade second-best in football and his design still adds fifth-most on top, the data's answer to "it's just talent." He shows the third-most-varied pre-snap picture in the league, deception by menu rather than by motion. His scheme fingerprint barely moves year to year, 4th to 17th percentile of drift, because a complete system does not need to adapt. Out of his heavy set he throws more than anyone throws from it and blocks with an extra body at 2.2 times the league rate. He is top five at making a surprise pay on coin-flip downs. And the honest wrinkle that ties the dossier together: his career number against the betting market is slightly negative, because the market already prices all of the above. The Jones season is the system's proof: the quarterback changed and the offense did not notice.

The catch: the QB comparison controls for nothing beyond the topline rates (opponents and game states differ), and "the system makes QBs interchangeable" is one team's one season plus a career of priced-in expectations, a strong story with honest limits.

Deeper into the vault, 18 August

Nick: "we have all this sumer data… can you come up with more creative ways to use [it]?" Three never-used field families, three new instruments: the player grades, the QB clock, and the free-rusher flags. One completes an argument, one kills a theory, one solves the Macdonald puzzle.

Scheme and execution, finally splitThe untangling tool

Michael: "It's so entangled that it's obviously very hard to know or tell"

Sumer grades every player on every play, and that separates the two. One trap had to be dodged first: the same-play grade is mostly the outcome relabeled (it correlates 0.54 with the play's EPA), so each play is scored instead by its players' grades on all their other plays that season. Then every caller gets two coordinates: how well his players play, and the EPA left over after situation and execution are both priced, which is design. Shanahan is second in execution and fifth in design; Ben Johnson fifth and third; both elite twice over, which is the data's answer to "it's just the talent." McVay sits at the league median on both, one more sign his value lives in the motion and the calls, not in a design residual. The quadrants tell stories on their own: Zac Taylor's design carrying an ordinary roster, O'Connell and Payton with good players and no design edge, Hackett at the bottom of both.

The catch: design value persists odd-versus-even seasons at r = 0.38, real but not settled as a trait; both axes correlate with career EPA, so they explain where a caller's EPA comes from rather than revealing new coaches; and the grade is three-level, so the execution axis is coarse.

The mental tax, tested on its own termsThe clock is a null too

Michael: "There's a lot more pressure post snap…and a lot less time…by shifting at this moment, it adds another mental task in a highly constrained environment. The later you can delay this, the better…(kirk cousins video)"

His theory is about time, so we tested time, and it joins the EPA null. Seven outcomes: time to throw, sacks, scrambles, throwaways, leaving the pocket, hurries, and time to pressure. After controlling for the real confound (rotated snaps simply see less play action and more blitzing), zero of seven move in the tax direction; pressure actually arrives slightly faster on rotated snaps and the QB does not hold it longer anyway. And his sharpest prediction, that play action should carry the biggest tax because the QB's back is turned, runs confidently backwards: the clock gap is smaller under play action, not bigger. One faint echo survives, thirty to sixty milliseconds against man coverage and blitzes, and it cashes out in nothing measurable.

The catch: time to throw only exists when the ball was thrown, so a tax that showed up purely as sacks and scrambles would hide from that one metric; that is exactly why the full battery runs on all dropbacks, and it is null too. Rotation remains what R/18 said it was: real, rising, stylistically persistent, and unproven to cost anyone anything.

Macdonald's pressure comes from nowhereThe puzzle, solved

The puzzle: he blitzes below the league's rate in all six situations, and his defenses finish second in the league in year two, twice. Where does the pressure come from?

From rushers nobody blocks. Sumer flags whether a pressure came from a rusher who beat his block or a rusher who was never blocked at all. Macdonald ranks second of 41 coordinators at manufacturing unblocked rushers, above what his situations predict, while ranking 24th at winning one-on-ones and 26th in blitz rate. The free rusher is also the more valuable kind by a factor of three: minus 0.50 EPA to the offense against minus 0.16 for a won rush, and a won block only even turns into pressure 62% of the time, because the ball comes out. A standard four-man rush springs someone free on 2.9% of snaps; showing five and sending four doubles that; actually sending five triples it again. Macdonald's result is the interesting one precisely because he gets his free runners without pulling that lever.

The catch: manufacturing free rushers does not confirm as a repeatable league-wide trait (odd-even r = 0.02, honest non-confirmation), though Macdonald's own rate has risen every season he has called a defense, 2022 through 2025. His story is strong; the general skill is unproven.

"Can you verify this?" The Seahawks two-high numbersClose, and the correction is a better stat

Michael: "Pre-snap 2 high…for Seahawks can you verify this? 78% pre snap the most in the NFL…? League average is 52.5%. For first down alone it's 84%" ... "They only play 6% cover 1 (29th) and 33% cover 3 (31st)?"

Checked against Sumer, number by number. The spirit holds, the specifics do not. Seattle shows two-high on 85.5% of dropbacks, second in the league, not first; Philadelphia leads at 86.7%. First down: 88.9%, again second. His league average runs ten points below ours on both cuts, uniformly, which reads as his source drawing the shell line differently rather than a Seattle error. Cover 1 agrees in direction (they barely play it) but not size, and the Cover 3 rank is flatly wrong: 16th, dead center, not 31st. The stat worth keeping is what the correction uncovers: they show two-high on 85.5% and play it on 56.2%. A 29-point spin-down, sixth-most rotation in football. The story is not that Seattle sits in two-high. It is that Seattle advertises it and then leaves.

The catch: Sumer charts the shell only on dropbacks, so a source that charts run plays too would read lower everywhere; that is the likeliest reason for the level gap, and it cannot be tested from this data.

The Rams out of 13, added inBoth his numbers confirmed

Michael: "it doesn't really make the case for 'Rams are dope at 13 personnel' - maybe add them in?" ... "I'm curious how good their pass/rush was against a light box and heavy box out of 13 too" ... "They were fucking nuts out of 13 personal. .50 EPA passing, .07 EPA per play running my god"

Both numbers check out inside our intervals (we compute +0.44 passing, +0.05 running; his tool counts garbage time, ours can go either way). The 2025 usage picture: nobody else is close, 26.1% of snaps against 12.0% for the next team, and their efficiency out of it ranks second behind Buffalo's much thinner sample. His box question finds the real story: defenses stack the box against the Rams' 13 personnel 72% of the time, and that is exactly where the Rams separate. Passing stays at +0.37 into a stacked box while the league sits at +0.03, and their runs hold break-even where the league gets stuffed. Those are the only two splits that clear significance. It is not that they are great everywhere out of 13; it is that the look defenses choose against it is the one it beats.

Going for 2Built, and Nick's hunch dies

Michael: "Do we have anything on 2Pt conversions? Going for 2"

Nick: "Correlates really high with 4th down aggressiveness so can probably combine them to an extent. Like no one is aggressive on 4th down and conservative on 2pt conversions. But I can test that actually"

Tested, and the quadrant that was supposed to be empty holds 28% of the league. Andy Reid, Dan Campbell, Matt LaFleur, Sean McDermott and John Harbaugh are all brave on fourth down and conservative on two-point tries. The correlation is r = 0.10 with an interval spanning zero, nowhere near "really high." Two honest wrinkles worth the script's time: coaches obey the go-for-2 card 99.6% of the time in the fourth quarter and only 48.6% before it, so the card is a late-game religion and an early-game suggestion. And two-point tries are so rare (10 to 19 chart-says-go chances per coach across eight seasons) that "two-point aggressiveness" fails its own reliability test as a coaching trait; the fourth-down version passes at r = 0.76. One decision is a trait, the other is a coin that comes up a few times a year.

What is a coach actually worthHis guess, put against three rulers

Michael: "I wonder how much a good OC/coach is worth… it certainly feels like there's an argument that they are the second most valuable person on the roster behind QB." ... "Like it 'feels' as if McDonald, Schanahan, and McVay are responsible for 3-4 wins a year. A WAR of 3-4 for the outlier guys"

The three measures on this board answer him with a ladder. Against draft pedigree, the loosest ruler, the all-time outliers do reach his band: Belichick +4.2, Dungy +3.0. Control for what the roster is actually paid and the ceiling drops under 3. Price the coach into the closing spread, the strictest ruler there is, and the sustained elite tier is about one win a season. The stricter the control, the smaller the coach. And the three men he named never clear +1.7 on any ruler; Shanahan's career number against the market is slightly negative, which is the measured version of "he keeps getting priced as a genius and performing to the price." His 3-4 win coach exists only in the measure that cannot tell coaching apart from roster-building.

The catch: "second most valuable behind the QB" is directionally plausible but unproven here; nothing on this board builds the QB-value ladder to compare against, and the one adjacent number we have (the QB index running roughly ten times the coaching residual in the leverage model) points the same cautious way. The market number is a lower bound by construction. Nick's next lead: recreating the Hermsmeyer scheme-coefficient model, on the ideas list.

The correction, from auditing our own chart: Belichick's +4.2 on the top ruler is mostly Brady wearing a pick-199 price tag. The draft model prices quarterbacks by draft slot, so two decades of the best quarterback ever register as winning without QB investment. Split the eras and the number confesses: with Brady he grades +4.9; post-Brady, on a 29-38 record, he grades +0.5 under the same control and slightly negative once the QB is priced by his actual play, both at or below the +1.01 market floor. The mechanism generalizes exactly as it should: Carroll shrinks with Wilson, Payton with Brees, Garrett with Romo. The ladder's lesson gets sharper, not weaker: the loosest ruler does not just flatter coaches, it hands them their quarterback's outperformance of his draft slot.

Same personnel, different playbook: what the Rams do out of 13, and what Kyle does insteadHis three follow-ups, answered

Michael: "everyone else kinda sucked at this while the Rams were insane. So what were they doing out of it?" ... "Wtf does Kyle do" ... "What ways do you think Schnahan adapted in 2025?"

The Rams out of 13 are running a different playbook, not a better version of the same one. Their runs are man and gap blocked at 40.6% against a league at 17.7%, they called zero power runs out of it all season while the league lives on power, they are under center 89% of the time, their play action fakes inside zone at four times the league rate, and they treat 13 as a passing personnel: lower run rate than the league uses it at, more deep shots. Eight of nineteen playbook metrics separate at p below 0.05, and the league baseline excludes LA so they are not graded against themselves.

And Kyle does not play 13 at all. Eight snaps all season. His heavy package is 21 with the fullback, a third of his snaps, from which he throws more than the league throws out of 21, runs outside zone at +16 points over the league, and uses less play action than anyone's 21. Two mutually exclusive routes to heavy: LA adds a third tight end, SF adds a back. And measured against the whole league's extra-blocker rate on runs, SF at 69% is the more extreme extra-blocker team (2.2 times the league's 32%), even though LA at 47% gets the personnel-innovation headline. Michael's "everyone else kinda sucked at this" also now has its direct test: league passing out of 13 is negative while LA sits at +0.44 (p = 0.0003), and league running out of 13 is well underwater while LA is positive. SF's version works less cleanly: his 21-personnel passing beats the league's, but his running out of it is statistically even with everyone else's despite the outside-zone identity.

And Shanahan did not adapt in 2025, which is Nick's point made measurable. Across a nine-metric scheme fingerprint, all three of his season-to-season transitions sit below the league's median drift for coaches who kept their jobs (4th to 17th percentile). McVay's 11-to-13 pivot sits at the 98th, which is what actual adaptation looks like on this ruler. Shanahan's stability is the signature.

What changed, and why it is betterRead this first

The approach, from Eric Eager via you: "I would start w what would the average coach do given situation and take the residual."

Win probability thrown away, per coachNew

Nick's ask: a leaderboard of value tied back to decisions rather than to outcomes.

Runs do work against light boxes, and light boxes are earned by passing more, not by running more. Wearing the defense down: does not happen. Late-game runs succeed at the same rate as first-quarter runs. Cold weather: the one kernel of truth. December football shifts things toward the run, modestly.

The catch: the box-count data only exists 2016 to 2023, and success rate is the metric there, not EPA. The direction is consistent across both.

3  Adjustments

Your first bullet, verbatim: "- Adjustments". Everyone believes some coaches own halftime. Nobody had checked whether that is a repeatable skill.

Halftime adjustmentsRepeats, but it is mostly team quality

Michael: "- Adjustments"

Found: the halftime adjuster half exists. Take every game since 2000, strip out mean reversion and garbage time, and ask whether a coach's second-half edge repeats across his career. It does: odd games predict even games at r = 0.48, and 44 of 159 coaches sit outside the noise cone where chance would put eight. Then look at who is on the leaderboard: McDermott, Dungy, Payton, Belichick, Cowher. That is not a list of adjusters, it is a list of good coaches, and the numbers agree: the "adjustment" score correlates 0.81 with plain point margin. Whatever this measures, it is mostly having the better team.

The catch: this test cannot separate "adjusts well at halftime" from "good team that also wins second halves." Isolating a true adjustment skill, if one exists, needs opponent and talent controls this measure does not have. And a single half is nearly all noise (the league model explains 1.4 percent of any one game); the repeatable part only appears after averaging dozens of games.

Seattle angle: Macdonald sits 46th of 170 with 34 games, inside the cone, too early to say anything. Carroll is 26th over 244 games and outside the cone, which under the confound above most likely reads "his teams were good," not "he owned halftime."

4  Game management

"- whatever else you think." The decisions coaches make with the game on the line, which is where the audience thinks coaching lives.

Timeout discipline, and the Andy Reid problemArgues with the audience

Why it is here: every fan believes Andy Reid is a clock-management disaster. This is the card that picks that fight on purpose.

Found: there are two timeout sins, and they belong to different coaches. Burning them early: Mike Martz is the all-time champion, one extra burned timeout per game over his era, double the league rate every season of his career. Dying with them unused in a close loss: Norv Turner did it in 11 of his 31 close fourth-quarter losses, 35 percent against a league rate of 15. And Reid? Four games out of 61. The best large-sample record in football at the thing he is famous for being bad at. Mike Shanahan is the only coach in the worst eight of both sins. The current-coach view puts Jim Harbaugh, LaFleur, McVay, and Payton on the burn list.

What a burned timeout actually costsSmall, and not proven

The follow-up question: fine, but does any of it matter on the scoreboard?

Found: less than the yelling suggests. Teams entering the two-minute drill with all three timeouts score 0.7 more points than teams with none, but most of that gap is clock selection, not the timeouts. Control for when they get the ball and the gap drops to about a quarter point across all three timeouts, which does not clear statistical significance. The line for the script: the sin is dying with them, not the price of one.

Icing the kicker26 seasons cannot prove it works

The belief: every broadcast treats the icing timeout as free psychology.

Found: across 2,221 pressure field goals since 2000, icing shaves about 2.9 points of make probability off the kick, and the confidence interval still crosses zero (minus 7.0 to plus 1.2). A quarter century of football cannot tell you it does anything. And no coach has cracked it: 20 of the 21 coaches with enough icing calls to evaluate sit inside the pure-luck cone. Matt LaFleur ices the most, two thirds of his opportunities, for an effect nobody can distinguish from nothing.

Pete Carroll, the exception that isn'tThe fluke you should expect

Why it matters: Carroll is the one coach outside the icing luck cone, and he is a Seahawk, so the video will want him to be a genius.

Found: he is the fluke the math predicts. Simulate worlds where icing does nothing and screen 21 coaches in each one: the luckiest coach in the screen beats expectation by about Carroll's margin roughly once a decade. His edge rests on eight kicks. This card exists so the video does not build a segment on a coincidence, and because "we checked our own guy and he is luck" is exactly the credibility the Seattle segment needs.

Wind savvy is a decision skill, not a leg skillAnswered

Why it is here: the quietest game-management edge we found, and nobody talks about it.

Found: on long field goals outdoors, attempt rates fall from 50 percent in calm air to 34 percent in an 11 to 15 mph wind, and that drop is the skill. The kicks coaches do attempt in wind go in at roughly the same rate as ever. The smart coaches are not employing better kickers. They are declining worse kicks.

Defensive coordinators blitz on autopilotRetired. See the 2025 rebuild.

Michael: "Ranking Coaches and play callers in some way" includes the defensive callers, who are in the attribution data too.

Found: pressure is what matters, and pressure pays the same whether it comes from a blitz or a four-man rush (minus 0.31 vs minus 0.29 EPA). A blitz that fails costs more than a rush that fails (plus 0.30 vs plus 0.22 allowed). So blitz rate should track opponent vulnerability. It does not: across coordinators, blitz rate is uncorrelated with the payoff (r = 0.06). Coordinators blitz who they are, not who they play.

Superseded. This ran on 2022-23 only. Rebuilt on 2022-2025 the relationship is r = 0.32 (p = 0.02), not a flat line, so the autopilot claim does not hold. See "So Josh Allen blows against the blitz?" near the top of this board.

Motion does not scare man coverageSuperseded by the 2025 rebuild.

The belief: pre-snap motion "reveals the coverage" and punishes man.

Found: across 31,468 charted dropbacks in 2022 and 2023, motion against man coverage adds 0.03 EPA per play, indistinguishable from zero. The deterrence story fails too: defenses play man 35.8 percent of the time when the offense motions and 35.4 when it does not. They are not scared.

Ran on 2022-23. Rebuilt on 2022-2025 near the top of this board: the main finding holds, the deterrence corollary is now a small but real 1.2 points.

Where this goes: a first solid brick for the deception video, and a warning that its intuitive claims will need the same treatment as establish-the-run got.
