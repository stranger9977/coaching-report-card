The Coaching Report Card

Michael's question: "Coaching is a tough one like how do teams grade that?" Here is what the data can and cannot grade so far.

Nick Gurol & Michael MacKelvie · August 2026

You asked how anyone grades coaching. It turns out I had been quietly building the answer since July, and none of it has made it into a text until now. The TLDR:

  • The best play-callers are more predictable, not less. Control for situation and the correlation flips sign. Andy Reid gets guessed three extra times per hundred plays and runs the best offense in football. Ben Johnson is the one real exception.
  • Your Seahawks hunch holds up three different ways. Vegas priced them at 10.8 wins, they won 14, third biggest beat in football. Madden rated the roster 23rd of 32 and priced 7.5 wins. Draft pedigree had them 19th, with the league's biggest gap between talent and results.
  • The warning label: beating the spread does not repeat (r = -0.03 across 654 coach-seasons). The market already prices the coach, so the roster models below are the better long-term measure.
  • The career leaderboard passes the smell test. Wins above what the roster predicts: Belichick +4.2 per season, then Dungy, Harbaugh, Payton. Macdonald sits at +1.5 on two seasons, real but preliminary.
  • Andy Reid is not a clock-management disaster. He has the best large-sample record at not dying with timeouts: 4 of 61 close losses vs a 15 percent league rate. The real sinners are Norv Turner (11 of 31) and Mike Martz (double the league burn rate, every season).
  • Dead hypotheses stay on the board. Icing the kicker: 26 seasons cannot prove it works, and Pete Carroll's apparent edge is the fluke screening 21 coaches predicts. Establish the run: zero in every metric, including its three best counterarguments. Halftime adjusters: the skill repeats on paper but correlates 0.81 with simply having the better team.
  • The strangest one: a roster's draft pedigree explains essentially nothing about how the team performs (r = 0.00 across 736 team-seasons). That is its own finding.

Everything below is one board. Each card is a line from our texts, then what the data says, then the chart that answers that exact question sitting under it. Open questions say so and name the next move.

Working board

Every question, and what answers it

1  The Seahawks question

Your third bullet, and the reason for the video: how much is a team overachieving based on its player talent?

The bit of magic, measuredHe is right

Michael: "I'm super biased. But there is a bit of magic to what the Hawks accomplished last year. Won without an elite qb…and seemingly without a lot of elite players. Very strange"

Found: the magic shows up in the numbers, and it is not small. The market, which knows the roster, the schedule, and the injuries, priced the 2025 Seahawks at 10.8 wins. They won 14. Only two teams in football beat their expectation by more last season, Liam Coen's Jaguars and Vrabel's Patriots. Whatever you felt watching them, the betting market felt it too, all year, and kept underpricing it. The roster version agrees: price every roster by its draft pedigree and Seattle sat 19th of 32, with the league's largest gap between what the talent predicted and what the team did.

The catch: "beat the market" and "well coached" are not the same sentence. The spread already prices the coach, so this measures surprise, not skill. The two cards below try to separate those.

Can any coach beat the market for a living?Mostly no, and that is the finding

Michael: "Coaching is a tough one like how do teams grade that?"

Found: one honest way is to ask who wins more than the closing spread says they should, career after career. Answer: almost nobody. Of 157 coaches with at least a season of games since 1999, ten sit outside the 95 percent luck cone, and screening 157 people at that threshold hands about eight to luck all by itself. Worse for the genius theory: across 654 coach-seasons, beating the market one year has zero correlation with beating it the next (r = -0.03). The names above the cone are the ones you would guess, Belichick with 23 extra wins across 387 games, Tomlin, Dungy, and now Macdonald, but the population-level result is that the market already knows.

The catch: a coach exactly as good as his reputation shows up at zero here by construction. This is a lower bound on coaching skill, not a measure of it. It is on the board because it kills the lazy version of the ranking and forces the roster-talent version below.

Macdonald career so far: +4.7 wins above expectation in 34 games, third best rate among eligible coaches, and outside the cone. Two seasons. The persistence null is the reason not to crown him yet, and saying so out loud is what makes the Seattle segment credible.

The roster could not explain it eitherAnswered, with a twist

Michael: "…like how much is a team overachiving based on their player talent"

Found: we valued every roster since 2003 by its draft pedigree, every player priced at his draft slot on the Jimmy Johnson chart. Seattle's 2025 roster ranked 19th of 32, middle of the pack, so "seemingly without a lot of elite players" reads about right by pedigree. Then they outscored opponents by 11.2 points a game, the largest gap between what a roster's pedigree predicts and what the team actually did of any team in the league. Underneath that sits a bigger surprise. Draft pedigree explains essentially nothing about team performance leaguewide (r = 0.00 across 736 team-seasons). Whatever separates good teams from bad ones, it is not the pedigree of the players on the roster, and that is its own finding.

The catch: pedigree prices the pick, not the player. A star who went undrafted counts as zero and a top-five bust counts as a top-five pick, and the "recent drafts only" version of the measure actually runs negative because losing teams get the high picks. Sam Darnold also had a bounce-back year Seattle does not get credit for separating from coaching. Say all of that in the script; it is what makes the flat line believable instead of suspicious.

Wins above roster talent, the career viewThe leaderboard passes the smell test

The ask: the WAR-style number, one value per coach.

Found: a mixed model over 736 team-seasons (2003 to 2025), performance against roster and QB pedigree, one effect per coach. The top of the list is Belichick at 4.2 wins per season above what his rosters predicted, then Dungy, John Harbaugh, Payton, McDermott, with Reid and Tomlin close behind. The bottom is Gus Bradley, Adam Gase, and Robert Saleh. Nobody fed the model a reputation and it recovered the consensus anyway. That is the check you want to pass before trusting it on anyone surprising. Macdonald lands at about 1.5 wins per season above talent, on two seasons, below the four-season bar the chart enforces. Preliminary by design.

The catch: because pedigree barely predicts performance, this "talent control" controls for less than the name suggests. Read it as wins above a league baseline with a QB nudge, not wins above a true talent measure. It also credits a whole season to whoever coached most of it (38 of 736 seasons had a midseason change). The next talent measure to try is on the ideas list: actual performance data, not draft slots.

The Madden version, since you askedBuilt, nine seasons

Michael: "- WAR based on Madden ratings or something…"

Found: we pulled launch ratings for every team, 2017 through 2025, from a cleaned public rebuild of the Madden data. The game does know something: a roster's launch rating explains about 19 percent of the variance in wins (r = 0.43), which makes it a real but weak talent meter, and that weakness is worth saying out loud in the video. Madden 26 rated the 2025 Seahawks 23rd of 32 and priced them at 7.5 wins. They won 14. The coach view over nine seasons puts DeMeco Ryans, McVay, Payton, McDermott, and Reid on top of the video game's expectations, and Doug Marrone, Saleh, Patricia, and Gase under them.

The catch: Madden ratings partly bake in last season's results, so this is not an independent talent measure, and nine seasons is a short window. Talent here means the average of a team's top 25 launch ratings.

Production note for the script: EA named two different games "Madden 25", and the ratings archive everyone links serves the 2013 one. The chart above nearly shipped with Calvin Johnson on the 2024 Lions.

2  Playcalling

"For this, idk if you have ever looked into or thought about how to grade play callers." Play callers attributed per team-game, 1999 to 2025, offensive and defensive, via the samhoppen public dataset.

The best play-callers are more predictableThe counterintuitive hook

Michael: "Topic 1 - Ranking Coaches and play callers in some way… - Playcalling"

Found: rank 80 play-caller careers by how guessable their calls are and the naive version tells the story everyone believes: unpredictable callers run better offenses (r = +0.33). That is an illusion of game script. Losing teams have to pass, which makes them predictable and bad at the same time. Control for the situation, down, distance, field zone, score, and half, across 305,827 called plays, and the sign flips: the more predictable caller runs the better offense (r = -0.37). Andy Reid gives away about three extra correct guesses per hundred plays and has the best offense of the era. Great callers are not hiding the play. They are running plays that work when you know they are coming.

The catch: this is a correlation across careers, not a play-level causal claim, and "predictable" here means guessable within a situation relative to league habits, not "runs on every first down."

Is it the caller or the quarterback?Survives the control

The obvious objection: predictable callers might only look good because they have Mahomes.

Found: the link survives controlling for quarterback accuracy at about two thirds of its strength, and it remains significant. Predictability is also a stable trait of the caller: it carries season to season (r = 0.48), and it carries almost as well when the caller changes quarterbacks as when he keeps one. It is his signature, not his QB's.

The disguise excuseTested. It busts.

The comfortable theory: good callers can afford predictable calls because they disguise them pre-snap.

Found: the formation data (2016 to 2023) says no. The corner of the chart the theory needs, callers who are predictable in their calls but hard to read from formation, is empty. The apparent disguise effect collapses once quarterback quality enters the model. The honest conclusion stays the uncomfortable one: the plays are simply good.

Every caller's tells, on one cardGreat video material

Why it is here: the fingerprint grids are the animation-ready version of the predictability story, one panel per famous caller, showing exactly where each one becomes guessable, plus the 2025 callers ranked so the video can be current.

"Establish the run is bs"Confirmed, in every metric

Nick, back in July: "Was just looking into some coaching stuff. Establish the run is bs"

Found: running early sets up nothing. A pass after a run on first-and-10 gains 0.015 EPA less than a pass without the setup, which is zero. Running a lot early does not open up late passing, and running well early does not either: the correlation with second-half value lands on zero whether you score it in EPA, success rate, or win probability. Meanwhile the map of actual game states shows passing beats running nearly everywhere the league still runs, by up to 0.22 EPA per play in late, close, first-down spots. The run matters in short yardage and almost nowhere else.

The asset: QUOTES.md in the repo has 18 verbatim, dated, sourced coach quotes preaching the establish-the-run gospel, with a documented exclusion list for the famous quotes that could not be verified. Ready-made for the cold open of that segment.

Steelman: the strongest case for the run gameTwo of three die. One survives.

Michael's standard, from the birthday video: "Not calling that out makes the argument less fair."

Found: we tested the three best arguments run-first coaches actually make. Light boxes: real, but backwards. Runs do work against light boxes, and light boxes are earned by passing more, not by running more. Wearing the defense down: does not happen. Late-game runs succeed at the same rate as first-quarter runs. Cold weather: the one kernel of truth. December football shifts things toward the run, modestly.

The catch: the box-count data only exists 2016 to 2023, and success rate is the metric there, not EPA. The direction is consistent across both.

3  Adjustments

Your first bullet, verbatim: "- Adjustments". Everyone believes some coaches own halftime. Nobody had checked whether that is a repeatable skill.

Halftime adjustmentsRepeats, but it is mostly team quality

Michael: "- Adjustments"

Found: the halftime adjuster half exists. Take every game since 2000, strip out mean reversion and garbage time, and ask whether a coach's second-half edge repeats across his career. It does: odd games predict even games at r = 0.48, and 44 of 159 coaches sit outside the noise cone where chance would put eight. Then look at who is on the leaderboard: McDermott, Dungy, Payton, Belichick, Cowher. That is not a list of adjusters, it is a list of good coaches, and the numbers agree: the "adjustment" score correlates 0.81 with plain point margin. Whatever this measures, it is mostly having the better team.

The catch: this test cannot separate "adjusts well at halftime" from "good team that also wins second halves." Isolating a true adjustment skill, if one exists, needs opponent and talent controls this measure does not have. And a single half is nearly all noise (the league model explains 1.4 percent of any one game); the repeatable part only appears after averaging dozens of games.

Seattle angle: Macdonald sits 46th of 170 with 34 games, inside the cone, too early to say anything. Carroll is 26th over 244 games and outside the cone, which under the confound above most likely reads "his teams were good," not "he owned halftime."

4  Game management

"- whatever else you think." The decisions coaches make with the game on the line, which is where the audience thinks coaching lives.

Timeout discipline, and the Andy Reid problemArgues with the audience

Why it is here: every fan believes Andy Reid is a clock-management disaster. This is the card that picks that fight on purpose.

Found: there are two timeout sins, and they belong to different coaches. Burning them early: Mike Martz is the all-time champion, one extra burned timeout per game over his era, double the league rate every season of his career. Dying with them unused in a close loss: Norv Turner did it in 11 of his 31 close fourth-quarter losses, 35 percent against a league rate of 15. And Reid? Four games out of 61. The best large-sample record in football at the thing he is famous for being bad at. Mike Shanahan is the only coach in the worst eight of both sins. The current-coach view puts Jim Harbaugh, LaFleur, McVay, and Payton on the burn list.

What a burned timeout actually costsSmall, and not proven

The follow-up question: fine, but does any of it matter on the scoreboard?

Found: less than the yelling suggests. Teams entering the two-minute drill with all three timeouts score 0.7 more points than teams with none, but most of that gap is clock selection, not the timeouts. Control for when they get the ball and the gap drops to about a quarter point across all three timeouts, which does not clear statistical significance. The honest line for the script: the sin is dying with them, not the price of one.

Icing the kicker26 seasons cannot prove it works

The belief: every broadcast treats the icing timeout as free psychology.

Found: across 2,221 pressure field goals since 2000, icing shaves about 2.9 points of make probability off the kick, and the confidence interval still crosses zero (minus 7.0 to plus 1.2). A quarter century of football cannot tell you it does anything. And no coach has cracked it: 20 of the 21 coaches with enough icing calls to evaluate sit inside the pure-luck cone. Matt LaFleur ices the most, two thirds of his opportunities, for an effect nobody can distinguish from nothing.

Pete Carroll, the exception that isn'tThe fluke you should expect

Why it matters: Carroll is the one coach outside the icing luck cone, and he is a Seahawk, so the video will want him to be a genius.

Found: he is the fluke the math predicts. Simulate worlds where icing does nothing and screen 21 coaches in each one: the luckiest coach in the screen beats expectation by about Carroll's margin roughly once a decade. His edge rests on eight kicks. This card exists so the video does not build a segment on a coincidence, and because "we checked our own guy and he is luck" is exactly the credibility the Seattle segment needs.

Wind savvy is a decision skill, not a leg skillAnswered

Why it is here: the quietest game-management edge we found, and nobody talks about it.

Found: on long field goals outdoors, attempt rates fall from 50 percent in calm air to 34 percent in an 11 to 15 mph wind, and that drop is the skill. The kicks coaches do attempt in wind go in at roughly the same rate as ever. The smart coaches are not employing better kickers. They are declining worse kicks.

Defensive coordinators blitz on autopilotThe defensive side of the card

Michael: "Ranking Coaches and play callers in some way" includes the defensive callers, who are in the attribution data too.

Found: pressure is what matters, and pressure pays the same whether it comes from a blitz or a four-man rush (minus 0.31 vs minus 0.29 EPA). A blitz that fails costs more than a rush that fails (plus 0.30 vs plus 0.22 allowed). So blitz rate should track opponent vulnerability. It does not: across coordinators, blitz rate is uncorrelated with the payoff (r = 0.06). Coordinators blitz who they are, not who they play.

5  Topic 2, Deception. Parked, not dead

"Topic 2 - Deception." Separate video. Our tracking-data access ends around 2022, so this one needs creative work off play-by-play and charting data. What already exists is below.

Motion does not scare man coverageA deception myth, already dead

The belief: pre-snap motion "reveals the coverage" and punishes man.

Found: across 31,468 charted dropbacks in 2022 and 2023, motion against man coverage adds 0.03 EPA per play, indistinguishable from zero. The deterrence story fails too: defenses play man 35.8 percent of the time when the offense motions and 35.4 when it does not. They are not scared.

Where this goes: a first honest brick for the deception video, and a warning that its intuitive claims will need the same treatment as establish-the-run got.
